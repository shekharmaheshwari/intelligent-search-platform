💬 Chat RAG

End-to-end documentation for ISP conversational chat — why it exists, how it works, full UI design, architecture, data flow, and implementation details.

1. The Problem with Plain LLM Chat

A large language model (LLM) like ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini is trained on a massive snapshot of the internet — text from websites, books, and articles up to a certain date. That training gives it general knowledge, but it has no idea what your store sells, what your current prices are, or which products are in stock today.

Without RAG

Customer asks:  "What plants do you have that are safe for cats under $30?"

LLM says:       "I'm not sure what plants your store carries.
                 Generally, spider plants and Boston ferns are
                 considered pet-safe and are often affordable."

Two problems:

  • The LLM is guessing from general knowledge, not your actual inventory
  • If it guesses wrong, the customer may look for a product you do not carry, or miss one you do

The LLM only knows what it was trained on. It has no live connection to your store.

2. What is RAG?

RAG = Retrieval Augmented Generation

WordPlain English
RetrievalLook up relevant products from your database
AugmentedAdd those products to the AI's prompt as context
GenerationThe AI writes its answer using that real data

The simplest analogy: a well-trained employee with a product catalog.

A new employee on their first day does not know every product in the store. But if you hand them a catalog and tell them "look it up before you answer," they can give accurate answers to any customer question.

  • Without RAG — the AI answers from memory (may be wrong or outdated)
  • With RAG — the AI looks up the answer in your product index first, then answers

Your Typesense index is the catalog. The LLM is the employee. RAG is the act of handing them the catalog before they speak.

Without RAG

            ← Generic internet advice. The LLM has no idea what

Step 3 — LLM answers:
  "Yes! We have three great trailing options.

   — perfect for beginners. Hoya Carnosa ($24) is
   easy to care for and produces beautiful waxy
   blooms. String of Pearls ($19) is a showstopper
   but needs a little more attention."

  [Pothos Golden $14] [Hoya Carnosa $24] [String of Pearls $19]

Customer:  "Do you have any bonsai trees?"

Step 1 — Typesense finds 0 matching products

Example D — Follow-up Conversation (Multi-turn)

Turn 1:
  Customer:   "Show me pet safe plants"
  Assistant:  "Here are our pet-safe options..."
              [Spider Plant] [Boston Fern] [Calathea]

Turn 2:
  Customer:   "Which of those is cheapest?"

  → The full conversation history is sent with Turn 2
  → LLM knows what "those" refers to (the plants from Turn 1)

The chat history travels with every request so the LLM can handle follow-up questions naturally.

5. How Vector Embeddings Power Chat Retrieval

This section answers: why does RAG chat use the same Voyage AI embeddings as search, and what exactly are those embeddings doing?

What is a vector embedding?

A vector embedding is a list of numbers that represents the meaning of a piece of text. Two sentences that mean the same thing — even if worded completely differently — produce similar lists of numbers.

"safe for pets"          → [0.23, -0.14,  0.87, 0.11, -0.63, ...]   ← 1024 numbers
"non-toxic to animals"   → [0.21, -0.13,  0.85, 0.10, -0.61, ...]   ← almost identical
"best gaming phone"      → [-0.55, 0.67, -0.21, 0.90,  0.14, ...]   ← completely different

The model that produces these numbers is Voyage AI (specifically voyage-3 or voyage-3-lite). It was trained on billions of sentences to learn that "safe for pets" and "non-toxic to animals" share meaning, even though they share only one word.

How embeddings are created at index time

Spider Plant product document
│
│  title:   "Spider Plant"
│  content: "Air-purifying trailing plant. Safe for cats
│            and dogs. Thrives in indirect light. Great
│            for beginners."
│
▼  Voyage AI embed_document()
│
│  vec: [0.23, -0.14, 0.87, 0.11, -0.63, 0.40, ...]   ← 1024 numbers
│
▼  Stored in Typesense
│
│  {
│    "id":     "plant-001",
│    "title":  "Spider Plant",
│    "content": "Air-purifying trailing...",
│    "vec":    [0.23, -0.14, 0.87, ...]         ← the vector lives here
│  }

How embeddings are used at chat time

Customer types: "What plants won't hurt my cat?"

Voyage AI embed_query()
    │
    ▼
[0.21, -0.13, 0.85, 0.09, -0.60, 0.39, ...]   ← 1024 numbers

This vector is compared against every product vector in Typesense.

The comparison finds products whose vectors are numerically closest to the question vector. Because "won't hurt my cat" and "safe for cats and dogs" were embedded by the same model, their vectors are close — and Spider Plant is retrieved.

The math behind vector similarity

Typesense uses HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World) — a graph-based index that makes approximate nearest-neighbor search fast even with millions of products.

cosine_similarity(query_vec, product_vec) =
    (query_vec · product_vec) / (|query_vec| × |product_vec|)

Range: -1 (opposite meaning) to +1 (identical meaning)

Hybrid search: vector + keyword combined

Chat retrieval combines both signals using Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF):

Customer asks: "monstera thai constellation price"

Keyword search ranks:
  1. Monstera Thai Constellation  ← exact title match
  2. Monstera Deliciosa
  3. Monstera Adansonii

Vector search ranks:
  1. Monstera Thai Constellation  ← semantic match
  2. Monstera Albo Variegata      ← visually similar plant
  3. Monstera Deliciosa

RRF combines both:
  Monstera Thai Constellation  → score 0.98  ← appears #1 in both lists
  Monstera Deliciosa           → score 0.71  ← appears in both, lower
  Monstera Adansonii           → score 0.54  ← keyword only
  Monstera Albo Variegata      → score 0.48  ← vector only

Why the same embeddings work for both search and chat

FeatureWhere embedding is used
Semantic search barEmbed the search query → find visually/semantically matching products
RAG chat retrievalEmbed the chat question → find contextually matching products to give to the LLM

If you change the embedding model, you would need to re-index all products with the new model. The LLM provider can be swapped freely without touching the embeddings.

Why the LLM never sees the vectors

Step 1 — Retrieval (Typesense + Voyage AI):
  [0.21, -0.13, 0.85, ...]  ← vector is used here, then discarded

Step 2 — Context building:
  "1. Spider Plant — $18 — Safe for cats and dogs. Trailing. Beginner-friendly."
                        ← plain text extracted from the product document

Step 3 — LLM receives plain text only:
  [system prompt with product text]
  [customer's question in plain text]

Vectors are a retrieval tool, not a representation the LLM uses. The LLM receives the same plain text description a human would read on the product page. The vector's job is finished once Typesense identifies which products are relevant.

End-to-end embedding flow

INDEX TIME (once per product)
─────────────────────────────
Product title + content
        │
        ▼  Voyage AI embed_document()
        │
   [1024-d vector]
        │
        ▼  stored in Typesense vec field


CHAT TIME (every message)
──────────────────────────
Customer message: "plants safe for cats under $30"
        │
        ▼  Voyage AI embed_query()
        │
   [1024-d vector]
        │
        ▼  Typesense hybrid search
        │   - HNSW ANN for vector similarity
        │   - BM25 for keyword match
        │   - RRF combines both
        │
   Top 5 matching products (plain text, not vectors)
        │
        ▼  Built into system prompt
        │
   LLM writes answer using product text
        │
        ▼  Streams tokens back via SSE
        │
   Browser renders product cards + text

6. How Semantic Search Makes Retrieval Smarter

Standard keyword search breaks when customers describe what they want in their own words rather than typing the exact product name. Semantic search understands meaning, not just words.

Keyword search vs. semantic search in retrieval

Customer typesKeyword matchSemantic match
"plants that won't hurt my cat"Fails — "won't hurt" not in any titleFinds pet_safe: true plants
"something easy for my first plant"Fails — "first plant" not in titlesFinds care_difficulty: beginner
"trailing green thing for a shelf"Fails — too vagueFinds growth_style: trailing plants
"low maintenance office plant"PartialFinds beginner plants + low light plants
"budget phone good for photos"PartialFinds camera-spec phones sorted by price
"phone that lasts all day"Fails — no product says thisFinds high battery capacity phones
"something like an iPhone but cheaper"FailsFinds premium Android alternatives

Semantic search means the retrieval step finds the right products even when the customer's words do not exactly match the product description. The LLM then gets accurate context and writes a helpful answer.

"pet safe trailing plant"
        │
        ▼  Voyage AI embed_query()
        │
  [0.23, -0.14, 0.87, 0.11, ...]  ← 1024 numbers representing meaning
        │
        ▼  Typesense ANN search (HNSW index)
        │
  Finds vectors closest in meaning:
    Spider Plant vec:   similarity 0.94  ✓
    Pothos vec:         similarity 0.91  ✓
    Snake Plant vec:    similarity 0.88  ✓
    iPhone case vec:    similarity 0.02  ✗  (different domain entirely)

Why multi_search is used for retrieval

Typesense limits GET request query strings to 4000 characters. A 1024-float vector serialised as vec:([0.23,-0.14,...], k:5) exceeds that limit, causing a 400 error. The chat retrieval uses client.multi_search.perform() (POST body) instead of documents.search() (GET query string) to avoid this. The two passes (lexical + semantic) are sent in a single POST body and merged via RRF.

async function sendChatMessage(query: string): Promise<void> {
  // 1. Snapshot history for request
  const history = get(chatMessagesStore);

  // 2. Add user message + empty assistant placeholder immediately
  chatMessagesStore.update(msgs => [
    ...msgs,
    { role: 'user',      content: query },
    { role: 'assistant', content: '', products: [] },
  ]);
  chatLoadingStore.set(true);

  // 3. Open SSE stream — include products from prior assistant turns
  //    so the backend can resolve pronouns ("it", "those") in Smart mode
  const messages = history.map(m => ({
    role:    m.role,
    content: m.content,
    ...(m.products?.length ? { products: m.products } : {}),
  }));
  const res = await fetch(`/api/v1/indexes/${indexId}/chat`, {  // indexId passed as prop per page
    method: 'POST',
    body:   JSON.stringify({ query, messages }),
  });

  const reader = res.body.getReader();

  // 4. Read and parse events
  while (true) {
    const { done, value } = await reader.read();
    if (done) break;

    for (const line of decode(value)) {
      const chunk = JSON.parse(line.slice(6));   // "data: {...}"

      if (chunk.type === 'products')
        // renders product cards above the text bubble
        updateLastMessage(last => ({ ...last, products: chunk.products }));

      if (chunk.type === 'text')
        // appends tokens to the text bubble as they stream in
        updateLastMessage(last => ({ ...last, content: last.content + chunk.content }));
    }
  }
}

14. What the LLM Does and Does Not Do

The LLM doesThe LLM does not
Read the retrieved products and write a natural answerSearch your database — Typesense does that
Format the response conversationallyKnow your prices or stock from training data

The LLM is a writer, not a searcher. Typesense does the searching. The LLM does the writing.

15. Why This Matters for Your Store

Problems RAG solves

Hallucination — a plain LLM will confidently make up products, prices, and features. RAG grounds every answer in real data from your index. If a product is not indexed, the LLM cannot mention it.

Staleness — LLM training data has a cutoff date. Your store inventory changes daily. RAG always queries the live index, so answers reflect current products and pricing.

Specificity — generic AI chat can only give generic plant or phone advice. RAG chat gives advice specific to what you actually carry, using your descriptions, your categories, and your prices.

Trust — customers can see the product cards alongside the chat answer. The product cards are the source. If the LLM says "Spider Plant for $18" and the card shows the same thing, the customer trusts the answer.

What improves chat quality

The chat is only as good as what is indexed. Richer product data → better retrieval → more accurate answers.

The primary lever is the chatContent JS extension assigned to each index. The extension runs at rebuild time and stamps a structured, labelled summary onto every document. This single field drives:

  1. The facet retrieval pass (exact-word matching against labelled values)
  2. The LLM context block (the text the LLM reads to answer the question)
  3. The embedding text (so semantic search can find attribute-specific queries)
IndexExtensionWhat chatContent includes
Plantschat_content_plants.jsPet safety, care level, growth style, light, water, price
Planschat_content_plans.jsPrice/line, data, international coverage, promotions, features
Phones / Tabletschat_content_phones.jsBrand, OS, form factor, price, colors, in-stock
Smartwatcheschat_content_watches.jsGPS, cellular, fitness tracking, compatibility, price
Accessorieschat_content_accessories.jsType, device compatibility, size, price, in-stock

To improve chat quality for an index: edit the relevant extension to add or reword attribute labels, then trigger a rebuild. No Python code changes needed.

Accessories device compatibility. chat_content_accessories.js originally derived "Compatible with: ..." by matching brand-level keywords in the product name (samsung/galaxy → "Samsung Galaxy") — a Galaxy S21 case and a Galaxy S24 Ultra case both produced the same "Samsung Galaxy" text, so chat couldn't distinguish which specific model a product actually fit. device_compatibility_accessories.js (@run_order 2, runs before chat_content_accessories.js at run_order 3) fixes this by extracting the actual device model(s) from the product name — the "... for Samsung Galaxy S24 Ultra" pattern already present in most titles — into a structured compatible_devices field, with an explicit device_universal flag for accessories that genuinely aren't phone-model-specific (USB-C chargers, Bluetooth audio). chat_content_accessories.js now reads these fields directly instead of re-deriving a weaker brand-only signal itself, falling back to the old keyword matching only if the compatibility extension isn't assigned to the index. Assign both extensions to Accessories for full model-level precision.

16. chatContent — Extension-Driven Context

The problem

Building LLM context from operator-configured field_config flags (chat: true) at query time had two costs:

  1. Drift risk — the Python loop that collected and labelled field values duplicated what a rebuild-time transform could do, creating two places to maintain.
  2. False-match risk — the facet_query_by pass required complex filtering (only facet fields whose type was explicitly stored in field_config, to avoid bool fields causing Typesense 400 errors).

The solution — chatContent field

Each index type has a JS extension (in backend/extensions/) that runs at rebuild time and stamps a single chatContent field onto every document. This field is a structured, labelled string covering all the attributes that matter for chat.

Searchable toggle constraint for non-string fields

Boolean and numeric fields (e.g. pet_safe: bool, price: float) cannot be made Searchable because Typesense's query_by only accepts string fields. The toggle is disabled in the UI for these types, and the backend rejects the request with HTTP 400 if attempted via the API.

The runtime guard in search.py also filters the query_by list to string types only, as a safety net for data inconsistencies.

17. Three-Pass Retrieval — How Attribute Queries Work

The problem

A query like "plants which are pet safe" breaks simple BM25 search:

  1. Typesense tokenises it: ["plants", "which", "are", "pet", "safe"]
  2. It tries to find documents matching all five tokens
  3. "which" and "are" match nothing → tokens are progressively dropped
  4. "plants" matches all product titles (high recall, zero discrimination)
  5. The word "safe" needs to match pet_safety_label — but that signal gets diluted

The same query also breaks naive semantic search because "plants which are" dominates the query embedding, and the semantic index only knows about title + content (pet_safety_label was never embedded).

The solution — three retrieval passes

Every chat context search runs up to three Typesense passes in a single multi_search POST, then merges with RRF:

Pass 1 — lex-all  (weight 0.3)
  query_by: chatContent, <extra searchable fields>, title, content
  query_by_weights: chatContent=5, extra=10, title/content=1
  Purpose: broad BM25 coverage of all text fields

Pass 2 — lex-chatContent  (weight 0.6)
  query_by: chatContent
  num_typos: 0  ← exact word matching only
  Purpose: precise attribute matching — "pet safe" → matches
           "Pet safety: Pet safe" in chatContent exactly.
           num_typos=0 prevents "plants" from fuzzy-matching "plant"
           in labels like "Growth style: trailing plant"

Pass 3 — semantic  (weight 0.1)
  vector_query: vec:([<embedded query>], k:15)
  Purpose: semantic similarity for general queries

Why num_typos=0 matters

Without num_typos=0, the word "plants" in the query would fuzzy-match "plant" anywhere in chatContent via typo tolerance (1 edit: "plants" → "plant"). With exact-word matching, only documents whose chatContent contains the exact token match — so "pet safe" resolves to documents where chatContent contains "Pet safe" verbatim.

Why chatContent is the right target for the facet pass

Previously, the facet pass targeted individual facet fields (e.g. pet_safety_label, care_difficulty). This required careful filtering to exclude booleans and fields without explicit types stored in field_config.

With chatContent, there is one string field, always present when an extension is assigned, always containing short labelled values ("Pet safety: Pet safe", "GPS: Yes", "Price: $45/mo"). No filtering logic needed — facet_query_by = "chatContent" is always safe.

RRF merge weights

PassWeightReasoning
lex-facets0.6Highest — precise exact-word attribute matching
lex-all0.3Medium — broad BM25 for general text queries
semantic0.1Lowest — used as tiebreaker; accuracy depends on rebuild

When lex-facets returns 0 results (e.g. query has no words matching any facet value), only lex-all and semantic contribute — behaviour unchanged from a two-pass system.

18. Embedding Enrichment — chatContent in Vectors

The problem

Without structured attribute text in the embedding, semantic search for "pet safe plants" could not distinguish:

  • Peperomia (pet_safety_label = "Pet safe")
  • Hoya Wayetii (pet_safety_label = "Unknown")

because neither label appeared in the title + content text that was embedded.

The solution

Because the JS extension stamps chatContent onto each document before the embedding step runs, _build_embed_text simply includes it:

def _build_embed_text(doc: dict) -> str:
    return " ".join(p for p in [
        doc.get("title", ""),
        doc.get("content", ""),
        doc.get("chatContent", ""),
    ] if p).strip()

For a pet-safe Peperomia plant, the embedding text becomes:

Peperomia Lima – 4" Houseplant                              [title]
Peperomia Lima is a compact houseplant...                   [content]
Peperomia Lima (Peperomia family). Pet safety: Pet safe.    [chatContent — starts here]
Safe for cats and dogs. Care level: Easy. Growth style:
compact. Light: Bright indirect light. Water: Every 1–2
weeks. Price: $12.99 (mid).

The semantic vector for this document is now close to queries like "pet safe", "easy care", "indirect light", "safe for cats" — not just the product name and description.

When the enrichment takes effect

The extension runs during rebuild. After assigning or updating a chat_content_*.js extension, trigger a rebuild to regenerate vectors. No field_config changes needed.

Fallback for indexes without an extension

If no extension is assigned (chatContent absent), _build_embed_text falls back to title + content only — identical to the original behaviour. The chat pipeline degrades gracefully; it does not break.

19. Follow-up Detection & Smart Mode

Multi-turn chat needs to distinguish two types of queries:

  • Follow-up — the user is refining or asking about the products already shown ("which is cheaper?", "compare it with Samsung", "does it have GPS?")
  • New topic — the user is searching for something completely different ("show me earbuds", "what tablets do you have?")

For follow-ups, ISP can pin the previously shown products into the retrieval result to give the LLM the full comparison context. For new topics, retrieval starts fresh.

Two modes are available, toggled in Settings → Chat → Smart Follow-up Detection.

Mode 1 — Heuristic (Smart OFF, default)

Three-tier detection runs in order; the first match wins:

T1 — Unambiguous pronouns / comparison phrases  (always follow-up)
  Regex: \b(these|those|them|compare|versus|vs|does it have|...)\b
  Example: "compare it with Samsung" → follow-up ✓

T2 — Anchored refinement phrases  (always follow-up)
  Regex: \b(i('m| am) interested in|i prefer|not that one|...)\b
  Example: "I'm interested in the red one" → follow-up ✓

T3 — Context-aware short-query guard  (domain-agnostic)
  Applies only when: len(query.split()) ≤ 5 AND prior messages exist
  Logic:
    1. Collect all words from product titles shown in prior turns
    2. Extract content words from the current query (len > 3)
    3. If ALL content words already appear in the shown product titles
       → it's a follow-up (user is asking about products they can see)
    4. If ANY content word is new (e.g. "samsung" when only Pixel Buds shown)
       → new topic, fresh retrieval
  Example: "how much is it?" after seeing Pixel Buds → follow-up ✓
  Example: "samsung buds" after seeing Pixel Buds   → new topic ✓

No LLM call is made. The filter is pure regex + set membership, so it adds zero latency.

For follow-up queries: retrieval is filtered to the IDs of previously shown products (plus any price filter). The LLM gets the same products it already summarised, so it can answer refinement questions accurately without re-ranking.

Mode 2 — Smart Follow-up (Smart ON)

Every query goes through an LLM rewrite step before retrieval:

Query + last 6 conversation turns (user text + shown product titles + assistant summary)
                    │
                    ▼  LLM rewrite call (same provider/model as chat)
                    │
         Standalone enriched search query
         e.g. "compare it with samsung buds" + history showing Pixel Buds Pro2
              → "Google Pixel Buds Pro2 vs Samsung Galaxy Buds4 Pro"
                    │
                    ▼  Free Typesense retrieval (no ID filter, price filter kept)
                    │
              Top 3 from rewritten search
                    +
              Up to 2 pinned from prior assistant turn  (_merge_cards)
                    │
                    ▼  LLM context contains both sides of comparison

When the rewrite is most useful:

QueryHeuristic resultSmart result
"compare it with Samsung buds"pins Pixel Buds only (ID filter)rewrites to "Pixel Buds Pro2 vs Samsung Buds4" → finds both
"does it come in black?"correct — pins prior productssame
"show me something cheaper"usually correct via T3 guardbetter if the cheaper product is a new brand

Cost: one extra LLM call per chat turn. Use when comparison accuracy matters more than latency.

Query normalisation — stripping conversational filler

BM25 search can be confused by natural-language prefixes. "show me pixel buds" sends the word "show" to Typesense, which matches product descriptions containing "show it off" (marketing copy), causing unrelated items to rank above the target.

_normalize_query strips common filler before BM25:

_FILLER_RE = re.compile(
    r"^\s*(show me|find me|can you (show|find)|give me|what are|i('m| am) looking for"
    r"|search for|look for|find|show|i want|i need|tell me about|do you have)\s+",
    re.IGNORECASE,
)

def _normalize_query(q: str) -> str:
    return _FILLER_RE.sub("", q).strip() or q

Applied in two places:

PathWhen
Smart ON, no history_llm_rewrite_query returns _normalize_query(query) instead of calling the LLM
Smart OFF, new topic_resolve_retrieval_params passes _normalize_query(query) to Typesense

Follow-up queries are not normalised — they are passed as-is because T1/T2 regex patterns need the original phrasing, and the query is used only to look up pinned IDs, not for BM25.

_resolve_retrieval_params — single dispatch

async def _resolve_retrieval_params(query, messages) -> tuple[str, str | None]:
    if _smart_followup_enabled:
        # LLM rewrites query; price filter preserved; no ID filter
        search_query = await _llm_rewrite_query(query, messages)
        return search_query, _price_filter_for_smart(query, messages)

    # Heuristic path
    is_fu = _is_follow_up_heuristic(query, messages)
    search_query = query if is_fu else _normalize_query(query)
    return search_query, _resolve_retrieval_filter(query, messages, is_fu)

Returns (search_query, filter_by) where filter_by is a Typesense filter string or None.

_merge_cards — comparison context builder (Smart ON only)

def _merge_cards(primary: list[dict], pinned: list[dict]) -> list[dict]:
    top3 = primary[:3]                             # top results from rewritten query
    seen = {c["id"] for c in top3}
    extra = [c for c in pinned if c["id"] not in seen]
    return (top3 + extra)[:5]                      # up to 2 pinned, max 5 total

pinned = products from the most recent assistant turn. For "compare Pixel Buds with Samsung Buds", the rewritten query finds Samsung Buds (new side) and _merge_cards adds the Pixel Buds that were already shown — giving the LLM full context for a side-by-side answer.
